# Age

The variable where the gap between what apps do and what the evidence says is widest.

## The headline, which is not what people expect

Older adults respond to resistance training. Not "somewhat". Substantially, in both
strength and muscle size, into the seventies and eighties. This is one of the better
established findings in exercise science and it is the basis of the NSCA and ACSM
position statements on resistance training for older adults, both of which recommend
progressive resistance training including heavy loading rather than gentle work.

So the instinct to hand a 55 year old a lighter, easier, higher-rep program is not a
safety measure. It is a worse program justified by an assumption.

*Confidence: high. This is mainstream and well replicated.*

## What actually changes

### 1. Recovery between sessions is slower

This is the real difference and it is a **frequency and ramp-rate** issue, not a
**volume** issue. The same weekly set count is fine; it may need spreading differently
and it should be arrived at more gradually.

Most pronounced after unaccustomed or heavily eccentric work, which is exactly what
week one of any new program is.

*Confidence: high on direction. Low on magnitude. I would not put a number on "how much
slower" without checking, and any specific multiplier in a program is invented.*

### 2. Connective tissue lags muscle

Tendon and ligament adapt more slowly than muscle does, and more slowly with age. Muscle
gets strong enough to hurt the structures holding it before those structures catch up.

Practical effect: **a longer ramp-in block and smaller load increments**, especially for
someone returning after years away. Not a lower ceiling. A slower approach to it.

*Confidence: medium-high on the mechanism, high on the practical recommendation, which
is standard coaching practice and low-risk if wrong.*

### 3. Power declines faster than strength

Rate of force development, the ability to produce force *quickly*, declines with age
faster than maximum strength does. It is also the quality most tied to real-world
function: catching a stumble, standing up, climbing stairs.

The correct response is counter-intuitive: **keep explosive intent in the program for
older users**, not remove it. Moving a moderate load with fast intent, not jumping onto
boxes. Apps almost universally do the opposite and strip anything fast out of an older
person's plan.

*Confidence: high on the decline. Medium-high on the training recommendation. This is
where I would most want a second opinion, because "make the 60 year old move fast" is
the kind of advice that needs to be right.*

### 4. Warm-up matters more

Cheap, uncontroversial, worth doing. More ramp-up sets before a working set.

*Confidence: medium. Widely recommended, less rigorously evidenced than the rest.*

## What does not change

- **Weekly sets per muscle group.** Volume landmarks do not need an age adjustment.
- **Rep ranges.** Older adults benefit from heavy, low-rep work. Do not push everyone
  over 50 into sets of fifteen.
- **The goal.** A 62 year old who says "get stronger" gets a strength program.

## The thing to keep in proportion

**Age is a weak modifier compared to training history.** A 55 year old with ten years of
lifting behind them needs a harder, higher-volume, more aggressive program than a 25 year
old who has never trained. If our algorithm ever produces the reverse, it is wrong.

The correct mental model is that age modifies the *rate* at which we advance someone and
the *selection* we start them on. It does not set the destination.

*Confidence: high. This falls out of everything above.*

## Practical, given we usually will not know

Age is optional and post-onboarding, so most plans will be built without it. That is
survivable, because the age adjustments above are all conservative-direction changes to
ramp rate and selection. A plan built for an unknown age should use the **cautious ramp
by default**, which costs a young beginner very little (a slightly slower first three
weeks) and protects an older one.

Put another way: the age-unknown default should look like the older-adult default, not
the young-adult one. The asymmetry of the costs says so.

# Sex

The variable where it is easiest to overclaim, so this file is deliberately careful
about where the evidence stops.

## Where the evidence is reasonably solid

### 1. Relative gains are similar; absolute gains differ

Trained as a percentage of where they started, men and women make broadly similar
strength and hypertrophy gains. The large absolute differences come mostly from
different starting points, not from different responsiveness.

Practical effect: **the program structure does not need to differ.** Same splits, same
progression logic, same volume landmarks.

*Confidence: high.*

### 2. Fatigue resistance at submaximal loads is higher in women

This is a real and fairly well-replicated finding, most associated with Sandra Hunter's
work on sex differences in fatigability. At a given percentage of maximum, women tend to
sustain effort longer and recover faster between sets.

Practical effect, and it is a small one: **slightly higher reps and slightly shorter
rest are better tolerated**. This is a nudge, not a different program. It does not mean
women should be doing sets of twenty.

*Confidence: medium-high on the finding. Medium on the size of the programming
adjustment it justifies, which I suspect is smaller than the fitness industry treats it.*

### 3. Upper and lower body strength differ by different amounts

Relative lower-body strength between sexes is much closer than relative upper-body
strength. A woman's squat relative to bodyweight is typically far nearer a man's than her
bench press or pull-up is.

This is the most actionable item in the file, and it is a **cold-start and progression
ladder** issue, not a program-shape issue:

- Default starting loads for upper-body pressing and pulling should be set lower for
  women than a single unisex standard would give, while lower-body defaults need much
  less adjustment.
- Bodyweight upper-body progressions (push-up, pull-up, dip) should **start further back
  on the ladder by default**, for the same reason as `01-bodyweight.md`: prescribing a
  movement someone cannot perform once is not a workout.

Getting this wrong in the generous direction is not flattering, it is a failed first
session.

*Confidence: high on the asymmetry. Medium on any specific ratio, and I would not hard
code a number without checking sources properly.*

## Where the evidence does not support prescribing anything

### Menstrual cycle periodization

There is a large and growing market for training programs organised around cycle phase.
The evidence base does not currently support it. The best available reviews, Elliott-Sale
and colleagues in particular, find the literature is of low quality, highly
inconsistent, and does not justify phase-based prescription.

**We should not build cycle-based periodization.** Not because it is definitely wrong,
but because we would be inventing precision we do not have, and doing it in a domain
where being confidently wrong is worse than being silent.

What is defensible, and is a different thing entirely, is letting anyone report feeling
flat and having the session autoregulate down. That helps everybody, needs no theory
about why, and the RPE work in `knowledge/principles/rpe-autoregulation.md` already
points at the mechanism.

*Confidence: high that the evidence does not support prescription. This is the current
mainstream reading, not a contrarian position.*

### Hormonal differences and hypertrophy programming

Frequently invoked, rarely cashes out into an actual programming difference that survives
scrutiny. Nothing here to act on.

*Confidence: medium-high. Happy to be argued out of this one with sources.*

## What this means for us

Sex is a **load and starting-point modifier**, in the same family as bodyweight. It
belongs in cold-start calculations and progression ladder entry points. It does not
belong in split selection, weekly volume, deload timing, or goal handling.

And, as with age, we usually will not have it. The safe default for unknown sex is the
more conservative starting load and the further-back ladder entry, for the same
asymmetric-cost reason: starting too light costs one easy session, starting too heavy
costs the user.

# Progressive overload

The single mechanism that makes any training program work over time: the muscle has to face
a demand it hasn't fully adapted to yet, or it has no reason to change. Everything else in this
folder (volume, RPE, periodization) is really just "how to apply overload without breaking."

## What actually counts as overload

Any of these, not just adding plates:
- More weight at the same reps (the obvious one, and the least sustainable long-term)
- More reps at the same weight
- More sets (more total volume)
- Same weight/reps but harder — less rest, slower tempo, closer to failure
- Better technique at the same numbers (more effective reps for the same joint stress)

A program that only chases "add 5lb every week" runs out of room in months for anyone past
their first year of training. Rotating which lever moves is what keeps it working.

## Rules for generating a session

- Compare against the trainee's most recent logged weight for that exact exercise.
- If they hit their planned reps last time: add load. Typical jump is 2.5-10lb depending on
  the lift (small joints/isolation moves get smaller jumps than squat/deadlift/bench).
- If they missed reps last time: repeat the same weight, don't push load on a lift they failed.
- If there's no history for a movement: start conservative. It's much easier to add weight next
  session than to walk back an injury from starting too heavy.
- Don't increase load and reps and sets all at once. Pick one lever per session per lift.

## Failure modes to avoid

- **Linear-forever thinking**: assuming every session should be heavier than the last. Beyond
  novice level this guarantees a stall or a missed rep, not a PR.
- **Volume creep with no ceiling**: always adding sets without ever removing any leads straight
  into overreaching. See `volume-landmarks.md` for the actual ceiling.
- **Ignoring missed reps**: if reps were missed, the honest move is to hold or slightly reduce
  load next time, not paper over it with "give them a note about form."
